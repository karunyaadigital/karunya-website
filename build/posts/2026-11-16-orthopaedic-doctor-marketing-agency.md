Knee pain, back pain, a fracture, a sports injury. Patients with bone and joint problems often search online before they visit a doctor, and many of them watch videos to understand what is wrong. That is why many orthopaedic surgeons and clinics now look for an **orthopaedic doctor marketing agency**.

But orthopaedics has its own risks. Surgery outcomes vary, recovery takes time, and the NMC's 2026 rules now limit how doctors can talk about procedures online. This guide explains what an agency should do for an orthopaedic practice, what it must avoid, and how to choose one.

## What an orthopaedic doctor marketing agency should do

The goal is to help patients understand their problem and find correct information about you. It is not to "sell surgery". A good scope usually includes:

- **Patient education Reels:** short videos on topics like knee pain causes, posture, when an X-ray or MRI is needed, and what physiotherapy does.
- **Scripts and shoot guidance:** simple scripts in Hindi, Hinglish or English that the doctor records on a phone.
- **Editing, captions and posting:** subtitles, clear thumbnails and a steady schedule.
- **Google Business Profile:** correct timings, address, services, doctor names and clinic photos, so patients in pain can find and reach you quickly.
- **Website pages:** one page per condition or treatment you offer, plus your qualifications and how to book.
- **Compliance check:** every post checked against NMC rules before your approval.
- **Monthly reporting:** calls, direction requests, profile views, website visits and content reach.

## Why education content matters for orthopaedics

An article for orthopaedic surgeons in the AANA newsletter (March 2026) notes that patients prefer educational content from their surgeons, and that medical facts and educational videos are the most preferred forms of content, with less interest in self-promotional posts. The same article warns that orthopaedic videos on some platforms are widely viewed but often of poor educational quality, which raises concerns about misinformation.

That is an opening for a careful doctor: clear, correct explainers stand out. It also says that infrequent posting is unlikely to bring meaningful results, so the agency's real job is consistency.

## NMC 2026 rules that matter for orthopaedic marketing

The NMC's *Guidelines on Ethical Advertising and Public Communication* came into force on 6 October 2026. As summarised by MedBound Times, they bar:

- Using social media or digital platforms to promote medical procedures or profit-based services, or to solicit patients.
- AI-made testimonials, patient experiences, or misleading claims about procedures or outcomes.
- Creating or manipulating a patient's image, voice, testimonial or clinical outcome.
- Buying, soliciting or manipulating reviews, ratings or search rankings.
- "Best doctor" claims; doctors are also advised against "number one" claims.

Educational content and health campaigns are allowed as long as they are not promotional. Practitioner posts should show the doctor's name, qualifications, registration status and register number. ## What your agency must never suggest

| What some agencies offer | Why to refuse |
|---|---|
| Patient walking videos after knee replacement | Works as a patient testimonial, which is not allowed |
| Before/after X-rays or posture photos | Before/after content is a problem under the rules |
| "Painless surgery guaranteed" or "100% recovery" | Guaranteed and misleading claims are barred |
| "Best ortho surgeon in the city" | Superlative claims are not allowed |
| "Free bone density camp" or discount packages | Free-consultation and discount offers are a problem |
| Paid ads asking patients to book surgery | Promoting procedures and soliciting patients is barred |
| AI-made patient stories or animated "recoveries" | Synthetic patient experiences are barred |
| Pay per lead or per surgery | Fees must not be linked to patient referrals |

## Good content ideas for orthopaedic doctors

These topics educate without promoting a procedure:

- Common causes of knee pain at different ages.
- Simple posture tips for people who work at a desk.
- When back pain needs a doctor's visit, and when rest helps.
- What an MRI shows, and why every pain doesn't need one.
- Myths about calcium, bone health and joint "grease".
- What to expect during recovery from a fracture, in general terms.

Each video should end with general advice to consult a doctor, not a booking push. Our [Instagram marketing agency guide](blog-instagram-marketing-agency-for-doctors.html) explains how this content workflow should run.

## Questions to ask before hiring

1. Have you worked with orthopaedic or other surgical specialities? Can I see their public profiles?
2. Who checks each post against the NMC 2026 rules?
3. Will every script and post come to me for approval?
4. How do you avoid patient faces, names and X-rays in content?
5. Do you send a crew, or guide my team to record on a phone?
6. Will all accounts stay in my name?
7. Is the fee a fixed monthly amount, not linked to enquiries or surgeries?
8. How will you handle comments asking for medical advice?

## Frequently asked questions

**Can an orthopaedic surgeon post about knee replacement?**
Yes, as general education: what the condition is, when doctors consider surgery, and what recovery usually involves. Avoid promoting the procedure, promising outcomes or showing patients.

**Can I share a patient's X-ray to explain a fracture?**
Be very careful. Patient privacy and the ban on using clinical outcomes for promotion both apply. Use diagrams or generic images instead.

**Is a Google Business Profile important for an ortho clinic?**
Yes. Patients with an injury often search nearby and call quickly, so correct timings, phone number and directions matter a lot.

**Should an orthopaedic clinic run paid ads?**
Ads that solicit patients or promote procedures are a problem under the NMC guidelines. Focus on education content, a complete Google profile and a clear website.

**How often should an orthopaedic doctor post?**
Regularly enough to stay consistent, for example a few times a week. Steady, correct content matters more than volume.
